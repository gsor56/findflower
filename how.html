How it works

How FindFlower works

Three steps, no account, and a confidence score on every prediction.

Identify a flower

Three steps,
start to finish

Step 01

Scan or upload

Use the camera, drop in a photo you already have, or paste an image link. One clear shot of a bloom or a leaf is enough, and no account is involved at any point.

Step 02

The model ranks what it sees

The photo goes to the inference endpoint and comes back with the most likely species, its closest runners-up, and a confidence score. A low score means it is unsure, and the runners-up are shown for exactly that reason.

Step 03

Wikipedia fills in the rest

Each match pulls a short summary and a reference image from Wikipedia, with a link to the full article.

Which model reads
your photo

The engine is picked once, in your dashboard, and the scanner reads that choice on every capture. Flora-Flash is chosen for you, and most people never need to change it.

Flora-Micro, on this device

A small convolutional model that runs inside the browser tab and knows 107 species. Nothing is uploaded, which is the whole reason it exists. The first scan waits while the model downloads; after that it is quick, and it works with the tab offline.

Flora-Flash, on the server

A fine-tuned Vision Transformer covering 116 species, served as ONNX on a CPU. The photo is uploaded, classified and dropped, and the five best candidates come back with a confidence figure each. This is the default.

Flora-Ultra, not yet

A wider vocabulary on the same server path. The dashboard lists it as unavailable because it has not been trained, and it is named here so the gap is visible instead of implied.

Both working engines choose from a fixed list of species. A flower outside that list still gets an answer, and that answer will be the closest thing the model knows, which is the other reason the runners-up are always on screen.

A first scan after a quiet spell is slow because the server has to wake up first. What that looks like.

Where a record
comes from

The model gives a name. Everything under the name is fetched from three open catalogues at the moment you ask for it, so a record is assembled rather than stored here.

Wikidata

The scientific name, the family it sits in, and the related species listed beside it. Also the poisonous flag, when one exists.

Trefle

The growing side: growth habit, how much sun it wants, and the light and humidity figures out of ten.

Wikipedia

The plate at the top of the card, the paragraph of description, and the link out to the full article.

A field with nothing behind it says so, in place of a guess.

A short record means the catalogues are thin on that species, not that the identification failed. Where two sources disagree, both are shown saying so. Nothing about edibility is ever asserted from a missing value.

Which catalogue supplies what, and the licences that come with them, are set out on data and integrations.

Corrections

What happens when you say it got it wrong

Every result can be marked right or wrong. That verdict is written to this browser and nowhere else. Nothing is uploaded, and nobody reads it.

The pipeline that would train on those corrections does not exist yet. When it does, it will be described here and on the releases page before a single record is collected. Until then the log belongs to you. Your dashboard counts it, exports it, and erases it on request.

New model versions in the meantime come from the training set, not from your feedback. Each one is written down.

Your photo

Where the image goes

The photo is sent to the inference endpoint, used to produce the prediction, and not kept afterwards. Only the species name and the confidence score come back. Nothing else about the request is stored.

Scans you save stay in this browser, and the privacy page spells out each of those paths in full.
